Dear Editors,

I am submitting my manuscript, "From Growth to Decline: International Student Enrollment and Regulatory Risk in U.S. Higher Education — A Descriptive Account from the Fall 2026 IIE Snapshot," for consideration as a research article in [Journal].

The paper sets the fall 2026 enrollment evidence released by the Institute of International Education — a 9% decline in total international enrollment, 15% in new enrollments and 15% at the graduate level across 809 reporting institutions — beside four federal regulatory developments affecting F-1 students in 2025–2026: the SEVIS record terminations, the fixed-period-of-admission rule (set aside by a federal court on November 16, 2026), the proposed $70,000/$30,000 OPT fee, and the narrowed reading of Curricular Practical Training.

The contribution is threefold. First, a descriptive reading of the newest enrollment evidence that states what each indicator does and does not measure — in particular the difference between the new-enrollment flow and the total stock, and the presence of OPT participants inside the headline total. Second, a classification of each measure by legal status as of November 17, 2026 (in force, under challenge, set aside, proposed), a distinction that public discussion routinely collapses. Third, an explicit statement of what the evidence cannot establish, together with the linked admissions-funnel, panel and cross-destination data that causal identification would require. The paper also states where its own regulatory-risk reading implies effects should be concentrated — in new intake, among students committing to long programs, in OPT-dependent fields — and why the published series cannot assess those implications: the comparison would need enrollment by program length at the level of individual institutions, which the Snapshot's public release does not report.

The paper does not estimate the effect of any measure on enrollment. It documents a decline, reports what institutions attribute it to, and argues that the pattern is consistent with a response to regulatory risk, while explaining why that reading remains untested.

Three points of disclosure. The economic estimates come from NAFSA, which is also a plaintiff in the fixed-period litigation discussed in Section 4.2; the paper treats them as an interested source and flags the projections as such. The account of the November 16 order rests on contemporaneous reporting, because the order itself could not be obtained in full; the passages quoted are quoted from that report, and Section 2 lists the other particulars that come from secondary sources. Generative AI tools were used in preparing the manuscript, as set out in the Declarations section.
